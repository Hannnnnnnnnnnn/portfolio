03 · Pre-order Experience

Designing an honest “later”

Turning stockouts into pre-orders without losing customer trust.

  • Self-initiated
  • Systems Design
  • Interaction Design
  • UX Writing
  • Front-end Implementation
  • Lifecycle Comms

Shipped solo, from problem discovery to production. No clean before/after data — and that’s part of the case.

Client
Atacz
Role
Sole designer and developer — problem discovery to production
Timeline
Feb – Mar 2026 · audited and hardened Sept 2026
Tools
Liquid, Shopify Flow, Klaviyo, Shopify metafields

Pre-order today. Your order is reserved and will ship within 2–3 weeks.

1 · The problem

Nobody assigned this.

Months before I joined, a collection launch sold far beyond forecast and shipping slipped badly. I read the aftermath in my first weeks — support threads asking the same question in different words: when is this actually arriving?

Store-wide reversals peaked at 9.4% of gross sales in October 2025, the month after that launch, and the highest point in the twelve months I could see.

The store had no way to say “later.”

When inventory hit zero the page offered two states: buy now, or nothing. There was no third option for a product that genuinely existed, was genuinely coming, and that customers were willing to wait for.

Demand arriving during a stockout simply disappeared. Demand arriving before one turned into weeks of silence, which customers read as bad news.

2 · What I found

The capability was already there. Shopify variants carry an inventory policy that allows selling past zero. Turning it on lets an out-of-stock item be purchased — but the storefront doesn’t change. The button still says Add to Cart, the page still behaves like a normal purchase, and the customer finds out about the wait after paying, if at all.

The gap wasn’t a missing feature. The backend already knew this was a pre-order. The front-end never said so.

Which meant this could be built without an app — no subscription, no separate checkout, no data leaving Shopify. Just the theme reading state that already existed.

3 · Decisions

Decision 01

Deciding what a promise is made of

Two conditions have to agree before the page will promise anything:

  1. Its inventory policy allows selling past zero. Shopify has to accept the order at all.
  2. This market, specifically, is out of stock. There is nothing to promise if it can ship today — and Canada and the US answer that differently.

It’s per variant, not per product. A bag might be pre-orderable in one colourway and simply sold out in another. Promising at the product level would have made the page lie about half its own options.

It’s per market. The same variant can be a pre-order in Canada while it ships today in the US, because the two markets are served by different locations holding different stock. A delivery promise that ignores where the customer is standing isn’t a promise; it’s an average.

Which is why the second condition needs a metafield. Liquid is handed one inventory number for the whole store, so the page cannot tell whether this market is out — the only number within its reach is the wrong one. So the answer is worked out where the per-location stock is visible, by a workflow that recomputes it on every inventory change and writes it per variant, per market. The metafield isn’t a note somebody leaves for the page. It is the market-aware stock level the page is otherwise blind to.

Selling past zero stays a decision, though. It is a per-variant setting someone turns on deliberately, product by product, and nothing is promised without it. The simpler rule — out of stock means pre-order — needs no configuration at all, and promises things nobody agreed to ship. Making someone say yes per item is slower to operate and correct.

Resulting states. Out of stock here, selling past zero → Pre-order. In stock here → Add to Cart. Out of stock with selling past zero off → Sold out, disabled.

{%- liquid
  # Market decides which per-market stock flag to read
  assign market_code = localization.market.handle
  assign key = 'preorder_' | append: market_code
  assign preorder_here = variant.metafields.custom[key].value

  if variant.inventory_quantity > 0
    echo 'products.product.add_to_cart' | t
  elsif variant.inventory_policy == 'continue' and preorder_here == true
    echo 'products.product.preorder' | t
  else
    echo 'products.product.sold_out' | t
  endif
-%}
Decision 02

Changing the button, not adding a note

The smaller change was available: leave the CTA as Add to Cart and put a line of explanation underneath.

Why I didn’t. The button is where the commitment happens. Someone who taps Add to Cart has understood they’re buying something. Someone who taps Pre-order has understood they’re agreeing to wait. Explanatory text below the button arrives after the decision it’s meant to inform — and on mobile it’s often below the fold entirely.

Changing the label changes the contract before the click, not after it. If the wait was going to become a complaint, this is where it gets prevented.

Trade-off. A word other than “Add to Cart” costs some conversions; some people stop at the change. That’s the intended cost. A purchase that happens because someone didn’t realise they were waiting three weeks isn’t a purchase worth keeping — it’s a refund with a delay attached.

Decision 03

Three jobs, one line

The line breaks into three clauses, and each one does a single job: name the action, remove the fear, set the expectation.

“Reserved” is the word doing the most work. It reframes the wait as something the customer has secured rather than something they’re missing. The item isn’t unavailable to them; it’s held for them.

A range, not a date. A specific date reads as more reassuring, and it would have been a promise made on top of a supply chain that had just proven unpredictable. A date that slips costs more trust than a range ever earns. So: a conservative window, deliberately wide enough to absorb ordinary variance — under-promising in the one place where over-promising had already been expensive.

Trade-off. It’s the same range on every product, regardless of what we actually know about that item. That’s the honest limit of a static string, and it’s the first thing I’d replace.

Decision 04

Saying it again, separately

Shopify’s own order confirmation fires automatically, so the pre-order email has one job: state again, in its own message, that this item is a pre-order and that it ships when stock arrives. It sends immediately after checkout, while the decision is still fresh. Silence is what turns a wait into a complaint, and the first silence starts the moment the confirmation arrives.

How it fires. The theme writes a hidden line item property when a pre-order is added to cart. Shopify Flow reads that property, tags the order, and hands off to Klaviyo, which sends the email.

Four systems, one signal — and that signal is the thing I didn’t make durable. More on that below.

One implementation note. The property is rendered conditionally rather than always-rendered-and-disabled, because disabled inputs still leak into the theme’s JavaScript form collection. I found that the hard way, and left the reasoning in a comment for whoever reads it next.

Decision 05

Deciding when a promise expires

Six months after launch, an order came through tagged Pre-order for an item that was in stock and shipped the same day. The customer was told to wait for something already on its way to them.

Where it came from. The theme writes the hidden property at add-to-cart, and that property never changes afterwards. It is a snapshot of what the customer saw in that moment. This cart had been sitting in the Shop app for thirteen days, from before the variant stopped being a pre-order. The theme does carry a cleanup script that strips stale properties — but it only runs when the theme renders, and a Shop app checkout never loads the theme.

What I decided. The property says what the customer was shown. The metafield says whether the item is a pre-order now. The tag requires both. That is Decision 01’s rule asked a second time, at the moment the promise turns into a commitment.

Trade-off. Re-checking live state can disagree with the page the customer actually saw. I took the stricter side deliberately: someone shown “Pre-order” for an item that turns out to be in stock gets it early, and early is a good surprise. The other direction is the one that costs trust.

Then my own gate produced a new false positive. I wrote it to accept either market’s flag — the property is already market-filtered, I reasoned, so checking either one is enough. An order placed on the US site and shipped to Canada showed that was wrong. The US flag was true, so the property was attached, the gate agreed with it, and the order then shipped from Canadian stock within days. The property is filtered by the market at add-to-cart time; fulfilment is decided by where the order ships. Those are two different markets, and Decision 01 had only ever named one of them. The fix is one line — let the shipping country choose which flag to read.

The shape of the mistake is the part worth keeping. Adding a condition to a gate only tightens it when the conditions are ANDed. Mine were ORed, so “check both flags to be safe” made the gate looser than checking one. I did not find it by reading the code. The reasoning behind it was written down at the time and reading it back revealed nothing. I found it by deliberately ordering the awkward combination.

The gate in the Flow’s Run Code, simplified 
// Where the order ships decides which market's flag to read.
// Computed once per order, not inside the line item loop.
const ship = order.shippingAddress || {};
const shipCountry = ship.countryCodeV2 || ship.country || '';
const marketKey = shipCountry === 'CA' ? 'preorder_ca' : 'preorder_us';

const isPreOrder = lineItems.some(function (li) {
  if (!hasPreOrderProperty(li)) return false;           // what the customer saw
  return li.variant.metafields.some(function (mf) {     // what is true now
    return mf.key === marketKey && mf.value === 'true';
  });
});

How I know it works now. Five test orders covering both markets and both cross-market directions, plus one real customer order carrying no property at all as the negative control.

The last row is the one that mattered. A gate that filters correctly and a gate that blocks everything look identical from the negative side — “no tag” proves nothing on its own. Confirming that an ordinary US order still tags is what separated them.

4 · What I can and can’t claim

What I can say. After launch, the “where is my order” thread that prompted this work stopped being a recurring conversation. Pre-orders became something the store could sell rather than something it had to apologise for.

What I can’t say is how much.

The flow shipped without a reliable way to identify a pre-order at the order level. The storefront logic and the email trigger both worked, so I stopped there. Months later, when I went back to ask what this had actually changed, I couldn’t separate pre-order orders from the rest. Store-wide reversal rates did fall across the period, but that data covers a window including a holiday season and pre-orders were a small share of volume. Attributing that movement to this feature would be a claim I can’t support, so I don’t make it.

Why it happened. This was one of the first features I built directly in the theme rather than specified for someone else, and I was measuring success by whether it worked. It didn’t occur to me that “did this work?” is a question you have to build the answer to in advance — that instrumentation is part of the design, not a reporting task that comes later.

What changed because of it. Order-level identification exists now, added months after launch — and Decision 05 is what happened when I finally checked whether it was correct. It wasn’t. More usefully, the habit changed too: on the PLP redesign that followed, success criteria and measurement method were written into the proposal before anything shipped, and the report was judged against them rather than against whatever looked good afterwards.

Still open. Two orders from August carry the tag with no property at all. The flow as written cannot produce that, so either it was edited after those orders or something else also applies the tag. Until I know which, the gate can be bypassed without anyone noticing. I would rather write that down here than describe the fix as finished.

5 · What’s next

Per-product delivery windows. Replace the static 2–3 weeks with a metafield holding each item’s expected arrival, so the page says what we actually know instead of the same hedge everywhere.

Tell them when it slips. A delayed restock currently reaches nobody until they ask. Once the date is data, the notification should follow from it.

Mixed carts. A cart holding one in-stock item and one pre-order has no stated behaviour — split the shipment, or hold everything? Either is defensible. Neither is decided, and that ambiguity currently belongs to the customer.

Expire the snapshot outside the theme. The cleanup script that strips stale properties only runs when the theme renders. The Shop app, POS and any headless path never load it, so a stale property can survive there indefinitely. Decision 05 catches the consequence at the order. Nothing yet clears the cause.

Consolidate the two code paths. An earlier per-product template approach still exists alongside the metafield logic. One implementation, one source of truth.
